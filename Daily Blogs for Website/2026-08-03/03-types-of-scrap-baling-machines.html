Types of Scrap Baling Machines: A Buyer's Guide to Choosing the Right Press

Ask ten suppliers for a "scrap baling press" and you will get ten quotations for meaningfully different machines. The category covers everything from a 15 HP press that fits in a workshop corner to a 650-ton industrial machine that needs its own foundation and a dedicated transformer.

This guide sorts out the types of scrap baling machines by how they compress, what they handle, and who each one is actually for — so you can narrow the field before you start collecting quotations.

First, the one question that decides everything

Before machine types, answer this: what does your buyer want, and in what form?

Everything downstream follows from it. Bale size is usually dictated by what your buyer's furnace or shredder accepts. Bale weight determines your handling equipment. Density determines your freight cost. A press that makes beautiful bales your buyer will not take is a very expensive mistake.

Then two supporting questions:

  • What material, and how much of it per day? Light aluminium behaves nothing like 10 mm TMT offcuts.
  • What is your site like? Available floor area, connected power, and whether you can pour a foundation.

With those three answered, the machine types below sort themselves quickly.

How balers are classified

Baling presses are grouped by how many directions they compress from, which is the main driver of bale density.

Single-direction (vertical) balers

Material is loaded from the front, compressed downward by a single ram, and the bale is ejected.

Best for: light, loose material — paper, cardboard, OCC, PET bottles, plastic film, fabric, foam, and light metal scrap such as used beverage cans.

Why choose it: the smallest footprint of any baler, no civil foundation required, low noise, and low power draw. A typical vertical baler runs a 10–25 HP motor and produces bales from roughly 20 kg up to 500 kg depending on model.

Limitations: lower density than multi-direction presses, and not intended for heavy ferrous scrap.

Typical buyer: retail and warehouse operations, printing firms, packaging factories, and any facility where floor space is tight.

Two-direction (double action) balers

Two rams compress the charge — one lengthwise, one from the top — producing a considerably denser bale than a vertical press in a still-compact machine.

Double action presses come in three loading and ejection configurations, and the difference matters more than it sounds:

  • Auto door — a PLC-controlled powered door at the charging box opens and closes automatically. Fast, hands-free loading for higher-throughput operations.
  • Manual door — a hinged top door with a front hand-lever. Simpler, more robust, more economical. The sensible choice at small to medium volumes.
  • Top ejection — the finished bale is pushed out from the top rather than the front or side. Solves the specific problem of a site with no front or side clearance.

Best for: UBCs, stainless steel, copper, aluminium, wire, tubing, white goods, metal sheet, corrugated scrap.

Why choose it: small footprint, no civil foundation required (so it can be relocated later), shearing blades handle material up to around 5 mm, and cycle times of 50–85 seconds on 15–20 HP.

Typical buyer: scrap yards, workshops and recyclers processing small to medium volumes who need real density without a foundation.

Three-direction (triple action) balers

Compression happens simultaneously from the top, the side and lengthwise. This produces the highest density per unit of machine size, and is why triple action presses are so widely used across mixed operations.

Best for: genuinely mixed input — offcuts, machine chips, auto skeletons, UBCs, wire, copper, TMT, and dry waste like cardboard and paper.

Why choose it: super-dense bales, full PLC touch-screen control, bale weights spanning roughly 10 kg to 1 tonne across the model range, replaceable HARDOX liner plates, and on some models no tying required at all.

Model range in practice: a Mini triple action press at 6×6" bale size runs on 20 HP with a 55–60 second cycle; a Jumbo Plus at 30×30" produces 800–1000 kg MS bales on twin 60 HP motors.

Typical buyer: scrap yards, rolling mills, packaging operations and recyclers who handle varied material and do not want a separate machine for each stream.

There is also a Mini triple action variant worth knowing about: a compact, heavy-duty press specifically for yards needing high-quality small bales from copper, aluminium and MS scrap, with three-direction compression, up to 10 tonnes/hr, online oil chiller and auto lubrication in a footprint that fits a smaller yard.

Balers classified by application

Some presses are defined by what they process rather than how they compress.

High density baler

Built for maximum compaction force across a wide range of ferrous and non-ferrous scrap. Cylinder forces reach 650 tons, handling everything from light aluminium up to heavy TMT bars. Bale sizes run 18×18" to 40×40", with HARDOX 500-BHN wear plates throughout and shearing blades that take material up to 20 mm. Top, turn-out and side ejection options.

Typical buyer: metal recyclers, TMT and ingot plants, scrap yards, casting plants, automotive.

Super jumbo baler

The heavy-volume industrial press — up to 300 tons of cylinder force, processing heavy TMT bars 5–10 mm thick and oversized scrap that standard balers cannot take. Production runs 2–10 tonnes/hr with bale cycles under 75 seconds, separate manifolds per cylinder and a HARDOX 500-BHN compression chamber.

Typical buyer: high-volume recyclers, large scrap yards, steel plants.

Car baler (ELV compactor)

Purpose-built for end-of-life vehicle recycling and a defining machine for Registered Vehicle Scrapping Facilities. Compacts whole car bodies, doors and bus or truck parts into solid high-density bundles.

Typical buyer: RVSF facilities, ELV plants, automotive dismantlers, scrap yards.

Automatic and semi-automatic horizontal balers

For recyclers handling large quantities of paper, OCC, PET, plastic, foam and textile rather than metal.

Automatic horizontal balers run the full cycle — feeding, compaction, ejection — with minimum labour, at press forces of 50–150 tons and up to 200 tonnes per shift. Programmable bale length, HARDOX 400 lining plates, and an online oil chiller allowing runs up to 20 hours.

Semi-automatic horizontal balers handle 16–24 tonnes per shift with automatic baling and manual strapping. Lower capital, still producing the high-density bales preferred for plastic processing.

Typical buyer: waste recyclers, paper mills, packaging operations, PET and textile recycling.

Specifications that actually matter

When you compare quotations, five numbers separate a good machine from a cheap one:

Cylinder force (tons). Determines what material you can compact and how dense the result is. Undersized force is the most common cause of a press that "does not make the bales it was supposed to".

Cycle time (seconds). Directly determines throughput. A 20-second difference in cycle time across a shift is a large tonnage difference. Ask what cycle time is achieved on your material, not on the easiest material.

Chamber and bale size (inches). Must match what your buyer accepts and what your handling equipment can move.

Motor rating (HP). Check against your connected load before you commit. This catches people out more often than it should.

Wear plate specification. HARDOX 400 or 500-BHN liner plates in the compression chamber are the difference between a chamber that lasts and one that needs relining. Replaceable liners matter more than initial hardness.

Three things buyers get wrong

Buying on price per machine instead of cost per tonne. A cheaper press with a longer cycle time and higher power draw costs more over five years. Work out cost per tonne baled, including power and maintenance.

Ignoring the foundation question. Machines requiring civil foundations add cost, add weeks to commissioning, and make relocation impractical. Several baler classes need no foundation at all — if there is any chance you will move site, that is worth real money.

Treating after-sales as an afterthought. A single-line yard stops completely when its baler stops. Before signing, confirm spares availability, installation and training scope, and AMC response times. Ask how many spares the manufacturer actually holds.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a double action and a triple action baling press? A double action press compresses from two directions using two rams — lengthwise and from the top. A triple action press adds a third direction, compressing from the top, the side and lengthwise simultaneously. The third direction produces a denser bale from the same material, which is why triple action presses suit mixed and lighter scrap particularly well.

Which baler is best for aluminium cans (UBCs)? Mini triple action and double action presses both handle UBCs well. Mini triple action is generally preferred where you want small, high-density, high-value bales; double action suits mixed light scrap where UBCs are only part of the input.

Do scrap baling presses need a civil foundation? Not all of them. Double action balers (auto door, manual door and top ejection variants), vertical balers and alligator shears are commonly supplied requiring no civil foundation, which shortens commissioning and allows relocation. Larger high-density and super jumbo presses generally do require foundations. Confirm per model at quotation stage.

How do I know what bale size to order? Ask your buyer. Bale dimensions are usually set by what their furnace, shredder or handling system accepts. Order to their specification rather than to a general standard.

What does HARDOX 500-BHN mean on a baler? It refers to the hardness grade of the abrasion-resistant steel used for the wear plates lining the compression chamber. Higher BHN resists abrasion from scrap better and extends chamber life. Replaceable liner plates are as important as the grade itself, since they allow the chamber to be refurbished rather than replaced.
