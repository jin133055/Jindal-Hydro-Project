Fodder Block Making Machine: Specifications, Output and the NLM Subsidy Route

A fodder block making machine compresses dry agricultural waste — straw, husk, dry grass and roughage — into dense, uniform blocks for cattle feed. The appeal is straightforward: loose straw is bulky, awkward to store and expensive to move, while a block of the same feed stores flat, stacks, and survives a truck journey without turning into half a load of dust.

The question that actually decides whether you should buy one is less about the machine and more about your feed economics. This article covers what the specification means, how to work out your own throughput from it, how the National Livestock Mission subsidy route works, and — the part most equipment pages skip — the situations where a block machine is the wrong purchase.

What the machine does, and where the value comes from

The press takes chopped dry fodder, or a total mixed ration (TMR) blend of roughage and concentrate, and compacts it hydraulically into a block of fixed dimensions. Blocks are then bagged for storage or transport.

Three things follow from that:

  • Transport economics improve. You are moving the same nutrition in a fraction of the volume, which is where most of the saving sits for anyone shipping feed beyond their own farm gate.
  • Storage becomes manageable. Blocks stack and can be held through a lean season instead of being fed out before they spoil or blow away.
  • Ration consistency improves. With a TMR blend, every block carries the same roughage-to-concentrate ratio, which removes a variable from herd nutrition.

The demand backdrop is real rather than promotional. In a Rajya Sabha reply on 30 July 2025, the Union Minister of State for Fisheries, Animal Husbandry and Dairying cited ICAR-Indian Grassland and Fodder Research Institute, Jhansi figures showing a national deficit of 11.24–32% in green fodder and 23% in dry fodder. That is a structural shortfall, not a seasonal one — which is precisely why conserving and densifying what is already harvested has policy support behind it.

Reading the specification

The JHP fodder block making machine is offered in two configurations. The catalogue specification is as follows:

Parameter FBM Standard FBM-HD Heavy Duty
Feeding chamber 24×18×43" 23×24×50"
Block size 12×18×5" 12×24×6"
Motor 25 HP 30 HP
Production 70–90 blocks/hr 90–100 blocks/hr
Block weight — dry fodder 10–12 kg 14–17 kg
Block weight — TMR 50:50 14–18 kg 27–30 kg
Machine weight 6.5 T 8 T

Both configurations are PLC-controlled with semi-automatic poly-bag packaging, and both handle straw, husk, dry grass, roughage and TMR blends.

Two lines in that table matter more than the rest.

Block weight varies by material, not just by machine. The same FBM-HD press produces a 14–17 kg block on dry fodder and a 27–30 kg block on a TMR 50:50 blend, because the blend is denser. Any throughput figure quoted in tonnes is meaningless unless the material is stated alongside it.

The step from Standard to Heavy Duty is a step in block size, not only in speed. The HD produces a 12×24×6" block against the Standard's 12×18×5". If your buyer, co-operative or transport arrangement expects a particular block dimension, that constraint should decide the configuration before output does.

Working out your throughput

Do this arithmetic yourself rather than accepting a headline tonnage. The formula is:

blocks per hour × block weight (kg) ÷ 1,000 = tonnes per hour

Running it on the catalogue figures, at the mid-point of each range:

  • FBM Standard on dry fodder: 80 blocks/hr × 11 kg ≈ 0.88 T/hr, so roughly 7 tonnes in an eight-hour shift.
  • FBM-HD on dry fodder: 95 blocks/hr × 15.5 kg ≈ 1.47 T/hr, so roughly 11.8 tonnes per shift.
  • FBM-HD on a TMR 50:50 blend: 95 blocks/hr × 28.5 kg ≈ 2.7 T/hr, so roughly 21.7 tonnes per shift.

Then work backwards from what you actually need. Take your herd size or your customer commitments, convert to tonnes of feed per month, divide by working days and by the hours you will realistically run the press. If the answer sits inside the Standard's envelope, the Heavy Duty is capital you do not need to spend.

Remember to budget for what sits either side of the press. You will need chopping or chaff-cutting ahead of it, and a mixer if you are producing TMR rather than plain dry blocks. The block press is one station in a line, and a line runs at the speed of its slowest station.

The NLM subsidy route

Fodder block making units are explicitly supported under the National Livestock Mission, and this is the single most useful thing for a prospective buyer to understand before approaching anyone for a quotation.

Under the DAHD operational guidelines, entrepreneurs and eligible entities are incentivised for feed and fodder value addition — hay, silage, TMR and fodder block production — with 50% capital subsidy on the project cost, capped at ₹50 lakh, released through State Implementing Agencies in two equal instalments. The guidelines specifically list "block making machines" among the machinery covered, alongside balers, TMR equipment, forage harvesters and heavy-duty power-operated chaff cutters. A Press Information Bureau release restating the scheme puts it the same way: 50% capital subsidy, maximum ₹50 lakh, for feed and fodder value addition units covering hay, silage, TMR preparation, fodder block making or fodder storage.

Points that catch applicants out:

  • Eligible entities are defined. Private entrepreneurs, SHGs, FCOs, JLGs, FPOs, dairy co-operative societies and Section 8 companies are named in the guidelines.
  • The balance has to be arranged. The remaining project cost comes from a bank loan, a financial institution such as NCDC, or self-finance. Self-financed projects require bank appraisal and a bank guarantee valid for three years, in the name of DAHD.
  • Working capital and land are excluded. The guidelines state that no subsidy is provided for working capital, purchase of a personal car, land, or the leasing and hire of land and office accommodation.
  • Release is staged. The first instalment follows the bank's first loan disbursement, or 25% project expenditure in a self-financed case, verified by the State Implementing Agency. The second follows project completion.

Separately, the Animal Husbandry Infrastructure Development Fund route offers loans of up to 90% of project cost for feed manufacturing units with 3% interest subvention and a two-year moratorium, per the same PIB release.

Scheme parameters, application windows and state-level implementation change. Confirm the current position with your State Animal Husbandry Department or State Livestock Board before you build a business case on any of these figures.

When a fodder block making machine is the wrong purchase

Being direct about this saves everyone time.

  • If you are feeding a single small herd and never moving feed off-site, the transport and storage savings that justify the machine largely do not apply to you. Better chaff-cutting and covered storage may solve the actual problem for a fraction of the capital.
  • If your feedstock supply is seasonal and uncontracted, a press that runs eight weeks a year is difficult to justify. Secure the straw or husk supply first.
  • If you have not identified a buyer or a defined internal requirement, do not buy on the assumption that blocks will find a market. Fodder is a local trade and the buyer usually has a view on block size and weight.
  • If green fodder is your problem rather than dry, this is not the machine. A dry-fodder block press does not solve a green fodder or silage requirement; silage and hay units are separate equipment, though the same NLM head of subsidy covers them.

What to confirm before you order

  • The block dimension your buyer or co-operative expects, and whether it matches the configuration you are quoting for.
  • Your material — dry roughage only, or TMR blends, and at what ratio — since this determines block weight and therefore your real tonnage.
  • Electrical supply against a 25 HP or 30 HP motor, plus everything else on the line.
  • Floor loading and space, given machine weights of 6.5 T and 8 T respectively.
  • Upstream equipment: chopping capacity, and mixing capacity if you are making TMR.
  • Packaging: the semi-automatic poly-bag arrangement, bag sizes and consumable supply.

Frequently asked questions

How many blocks per hour does a fodder block making machine produce? The JHP range is specified at 70–90 blocks per hour for the FBM Standard and 90–100 blocks per hour for the FBM-HD Heavy Duty. Convert that to tonnes using the block weight for your material, since a dry fodder block and a TMR 50:50 block weigh very differently on the same machine.

What size blocks does the machine make? The FBM Standard produces a 12×18×5" block; the FBM-HD Heavy Duty produces a 12×24×6" block. Confirm which dimension your buyer or co-operative accepts before choosing a configuration, because this is usually a harder constraint than throughput.

Is there a government subsidy for a fodder block making unit in India? Yes. Under the National Livestock Mission, feed and fodder value addition units — including fodder block making — are eligible for 50% capital subsidy on project cost, capped at ₹50 lakh, released in two instalments through State Implementing Agencies. Working capital, land and leasing are excluded. Scheme terms change and implementation is state-level, so confirm with your State Animal Husbandry Department.

Can the same machine make TMR blocks and plain dry fodder blocks? Both configurations are specified to handle dry fodder and TMR 50:50 blends. The difference shows up in block weight — the FBM-HD produces 14–17 kg on dry fodder and 27–30 kg on a TMR blend — so plan your bagging and handling around the heavier case.

What power supply does a fodder block making machine need? The FBM Standard runs a 25 HP motor and the FBM-HD a 30 HP motor. Size your connection for the whole line, not the press alone, since chaff cutters and mixers upstream draw their own load. Check your sanctioned load before ordering, as an upgrade can take longer than the machine delivery.
