Second Hand Baling Press Machine for Sale: What to Inspect, and When to Walk Away

Looking at a second hand baling press machine for sale is a reasonable thing to do. A used press can cut your capital outlay substantially and get you running in weeks rather than months. It can also be a machine somebody is selling precisely because it stopped paying for itself — and you will not find that out from a photograph.

The difference between those two outcomes is inspection. This article is the checklist: what to look at, what to ask, what the asking price leaves out, and when buying used is the wrong call even at a good price.

We manufacture new machines, so read this with that in mind. But a well-inspected used press suits some buyers better than a new one, and we would rather you knew which category you are in.

When buying used genuinely makes sense

Four situations, and they are real ones.

You are testing a business, not committing to it. If you are not yet certain baling is worth doing at your volume, a used machine buys you the answer cheaply. Find out on somebody else's depreciation.

Your volume is low and stable. A press running two hours a day will take a very long time to wear out, whatever its history. Utilisation, not age, is what consumes a machine.

The material is undemanding. Light sheet, aluminium sections, UBCs, cardboard. A press handling gentle material has had an easier life and will continue to.

You need it next week. New machines are built to order. If a contract starts on the 1st, a machine you can inspect and collect has value that a better machine in eight weeks does not.

One qualifier applies to all four: they hold only if you can inspect the machine physically and run it under load. A used press bought from photographs is not a cheaper machine, it is a lottery ticket.

When it does not

Heavy or abrasive material. If you are baling TMT, heavy structural steel or mixed ferrous, the machine has worked hard for its whole life and the wear surfaces are effectively the whole machine. A used press for this duty is usually a rebuild pretending to be a purchase.

The bale spec is contractual. If your buyer specifies bale dimensions and density, you need a press that hits them repeatably. A worn chamber and tired hydraulics will not, and you find out after the first rejected load.

High utilisation. Two shifts a day makes downtime expensive. On a machine of unknown provenance you can neither predict downtime nor source parts quickly — the worst combination there is.

You cannot identify the original manufacturer. No maker, no drawings, no part numbers, no support. Every future repair becomes bespoke fabrication. This alone is enough to walk away.

The seller will not let you run it. There is only one reason for that.

The inspection: what to actually look at

Take a torch, a magnet, a tape measure and — if you can — someone who has maintained a hydraulic press. Budget two hours.

Hydraulics

This is where the money is, so start here.

  • Oil condition. Draw a sample. Milky means water ingress. Dark and varnished means it has run hot for a long time. Gritty between your fingers means metal in the system, which means pump wear.
  • Cylinder rods. Look for scoring, pitting and rust on the exposed rod. A scored rod eats seals continuously, and re-chroming or replacing a large cylinder is a significant cost.
  • Drift test. Cycle the ram out, stop, and watch it for five minutes. Any creep means internal leakage past the piston seals.
  • Pressure under load. Ask to see the gauge with the chamber charged, not empty. Compare against the machine's rated working pressure.
  • Leaks. Some weeping at fittings is normal on an older machine. Wet patches under the power pack are not.
  • Temperature. Run it for twenty minutes and put your hand near the tank. A press running hot at low duty will run hotter at yours. Ask whether it has an oil chiller — several machines in the JHP range are specified with one for long-hour operation.

Structure and wear surfaces

  • Chamber liner plates. The single most important wear item. Measure remaining thickness if you can, and look for gouging and dishing. Ask what grade they are and whether they are replaceable — replaceable HARDOX liner plates are standard on the JHP Triple Action Baler, and a chamber with non-replaceable worn liners is a much bigger problem than one with bolt-in plates.
  • Ram face and guides. Uneven wear points to misalignment, which will keep recurring after you fix it.
  • Shearing blades. Check edge condition and ask what material thickness they are rated for. The JHP catalogue specifies up to 20mm on the High Density Baler and up to 5mm on the Double Action machines — very different capability, and a seller vague about which is which is guessing.
  • Frame welds. Look for cracks and — worse — repair welds. A repaired frame crack tells you the machine has been loaded beyond design at some point.
  • Door and locking mechanism. Watch it close under power. Play in the door means bales that fail dimension.

Electricals, controls and safety

  • Panel condition. Open it. Dust and heat kill contactors. Non-standard rewiring means the original documentation no longer describes the machine.
  • PLC and HMI. Find out whether the model is still supported. An obsolete PLC with no spare is a machine waiting to become scrap.
  • Motor. Check the nameplate rating against your sanctioned load, and listen to it start.
  • Safety interlocks and emergency stops. Test every one. If any has been bypassed, assume they all have been, and price in a full safety refit.

Documentation

Ask for the original manual and electrical drawings, the maker's name and model number, purchase invoice and GST records, and any service history. The service history is usually the weakest link, and its absence is itself information.

The costs that are not in the asking price

Build your comparison on the installed, running cost — not the sticker.

  • Transport and rigging. A heavy, awkward load. Get a firm quote including craneage at both ends before you commit.
  • Refurbishment. Seal kits, hoses, liner plates, blades, contactors — priced from your inspection findings, not optimistically.
  • Foundation and civil work. Check whether the machine needs one. Some presses do not — the JHP Double Action variants and Vertical Baler are specified as requiring no civil foundation — but if yours does, that is weeks and money.
  • Electrical connection. Motor rating against sanctioned load, plus cabling and panel work.
  • Commissioning. On a new machine this is typically included with operator training. On a used one it usually is not.
  • Spares availability. The cost that bites later. Ask whether the maker still supports the model and how long a common part takes to arrive. A six-week lead time on seal kits is not the machine you think you bought.
  • Safety compliance. Guarding and interlocks to current practice, if absent.
  • No warranty, usually. Some refurbishers offer a limited one. Read exactly what it covers.

How to compare properly. Add purchase price, transport, refurbishment, civil and electrical work, and commissioning to get an installed cost. Divide by the tonnes you realistically expect to bale over the years you expect to keep it, and add estimated maintenance per year. Do the same arithmetic for a new machine using a real quotation and the supplier's stated cycle time. Then compare cost per tonne, not cost per machine. We will not invent numbers here — the figures that matter are your material, your volume and your quotations. But the method is the same in every case, and it frequently changes the answer.

If the machine is imported

A used press from outside India carries a compliance load a domestic purchase does not. Second-hand capital goods are generally importable under the "free" category, but customs will not clear the shipment without a Chartered Engineer's certificate covering condition, year of manufacture and residual life — get it issued overseas at the load port, because arranging it after arrival invites full examination and demurrage while the machine sits at the port (TaxGuru).

Valuation is the second trap. Customs may reject your invoice price and assess duty on a depreciated value derived from the machine's value when new; CBIC Circular No. 07/2020-Customs sets out the sequence in which the Customs Valuation Rules 2007 apply to second-hand machinery (IndiaFilings). Make sure the certificate states the value in the year of manufacture — if it does not, customs will estimate it, rarely in your favour. Also check whether the category falls under a Quality Control Order requiring BIS certification, which applies to used equipment too.

These rules change. Confirm the current position with DGFT, CBIC and your customs broker before you commit — this is a description of the landscape, not clearance advice.

One question to ask early

Reputable refurbishers exist, and there is an active used market in India (Machineseeker India). The good sellers let you run the machine and hand over the maker's details without being pushed.

So ask: why is this machine for sale? Plant closure, capacity upgrade and business exit are ordinary answers. "It wasn't suitable for our material" is worth pursuing — it may not be suitable for yours either.

Frequently asked questions

Is it worth buying a second hand baling press machine? It depends on utilisation and material. A used press is a sound buy if your volume is low and stable, your material is light, you are testing whether baling pays at all, or you need a machine immediately — and if you can inspect it and run it under load. It is a poor buy for heavy or abrasive material, contractual bale specifications, two-shift running, or any machine whose maker you cannot identify.

What should I inspect on a used baling press? Start with the hydraulics: oil condition, cylinder rod scoring, a five-minute drift test for internal leakage, working pressure under load, and running temperature. Then chamber liner plates and whether they are replaceable, ram and guide wear, blade condition, frame and repair welds, and door play. Finish with the electrical panel, PLC support status, motor nameplate and every safety interlock.

How much cheaper is a used baler than a new one? Sticker price is not the comparison that matters. Add transport, refurbishment, civil and electrical work and commissioning to get an installed cost, divide by the tonnes you expect to process over the machine's remaining life, and add annual maintenance. Do the same with a real quotation for a new machine and compare cost per tonne. Anyone quoting a universal percentage saving is guessing.

What are the risks of importing a used baling press into India? Two main ones. Customs will not clear the machine without a Chartered Engineer's certificate covering condition, year of manufacture and residual life — arrange it at the load port, not on arrival. And customs may assess duty on a depreciated value rather than your invoice price, under the sequence in CBIC Circular No. 07/2020-Customs. Also check for a BIS Quality Control Order. Confirm current requirements with DGFT and your customs broker.

Can I get spares for a second hand baling press? Only if you can identify the original manufacturer and that manufacturer still supports the model. Establish this before you buy — ask what a seal kit or solenoid valve costs and how long it takes to arrive. A machine with no identifiable maker turns every future repair into bespoke fabrication.

What does a used baling press cost to refurbish? There is no standard figure; it depends on what your inspection finds. Price the specific items you identified — seal kits, hoses, liner plates, blades, contactors, guarding — from actual quotations before agreeing a purchase price, and treat anything you could not inspect as an unknown rather than as zero.
