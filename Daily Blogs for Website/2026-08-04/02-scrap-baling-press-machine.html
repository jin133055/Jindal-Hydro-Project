Scrap Baling Press Machine: How to Read the Spec Sheet and Size the Press

Every scrap baling press machine is sold with a specification table, and almost nobody reads it properly. Buyers look at two numbers — bale size and motor HP — and treat everything else as manufacturer detail. Then the press arrives and produces bales half the weight they budgeted for, at a cycle time that will not hit the tonnage they promised their buyer.

The specification table is not decoration. It is the machine's honest description, and once you know what each column means you can size a press yourself before you speak to anyone. This article decodes each line, then gives you a sizing method you can run on the back of an envelope.

Chamber size and bale size are different numbers

This is the single most common misreading. A specification will list, for example, a chamber of 76×40×30 inches producing an 18×18-inch bale. Those numbers are not in conflict — the chamber is the box you load scrap into, and the bale is what comes out after compaction from one, two or three directions.

The gap between them is the compaction ratio, and it is where the press does its work. In the Jindal Hydro Projects High Density Baler range the ladder runs like this:

Bale size (in) Chamber (in) Bale weight, MS (kg) Cycle (sec) Motor (HP)
18×18 76×40×30 120–140 50–55 60/80
20×20 / 22×22 84×44×36 250–350 80–85 80/100
24×24 84×60×40 450–600 80–120 80/100/120
28×28 / 35×35 105×60×50 700–800 90–100 80/100/120
40×40 110×60×55 800–1000 90–100 80/100/120

Read it in the right direction. You do not choose a chamber — you choose the bale your buyer accepts, and the chamber follows. If your trader or furnace wants a specific bale format, that decision is already made for you, and shopping for a bigger press is shopping for a bale nobody wants to buy.

Bale weight depends on what you are baling

Notice that the table above says "MS" — mild steel. That qualifier matters more than any other word on a spec sheet.

The Triple Action Baler makes the point starkly. On the Mini 6×6 model, the catalogue lists a bale weight of 10–16 kg in mild steel and 4–6 kg in aluminium. On the Jumbo Plus 30×30 it is 800–1000 kg in mild steel against 250–350 kg in aluminium. Same machine, same cycle, roughly a third of the weight.

That is density, not a defect. Aluminium is about a third the density of steel, so a press that fills the same box with the same force produces a much lighter bale. If you are baling UBCs, aluminium sections or light copper and you budget on the mild steel column, your tonnage forecast will be wrong by a factor of three.

The same applies on the waste side. The Automatic Horizontal Baler quotes bale weights in OCC — old corrugated cardboard — and the Semi-Automatic Horizontal Baler quotes both OCC and PET separately, because PET bottles and cardboard fill a chamber very differently. A 35×29 bale is listed at 600–750 kg in OCC and 350–600 kg in PET.

Ask for the bale weight on your material. If the specification does not cover it, that is a reasonable question to put to the manufacturer, not a detail to assume.

Cycle time is throughput; motor rating is not

Motor HP tells you connected load and roughly what your electricity bill will look like. It does not tell you tonnes per hour. Cycle time does.

The arithmetic is simple. Bale weight divided by cycle time, scaled up, is your theoretical throughput:

  • Jumbo Plus 30×30 Triple Action, 800–1000 kg MS at 90–100 seconds: about 36–40 bales an hour, roughly 29–40 tonnes an hour theoretical.
  • High Density 18×18, 120–140 kg at 50–55 seconds: about 68 bales an hour, roughly 8–9 tonnes an hour theoretical.

Then discount it heavily. Theoretical cycle time assumes the chamber is full and the material is ready. In a real yard you lose time to loading, sorting, strapping, bale removal, breaks and stoppages. A realistic planning figure is well below the theoretical maximum — which is why some machines are specified on production rather than cycle. The Super Jumbo Baler is listed at 24–30 tonnes per 8-hour shift for a 20×20 bale and 62–72 tonnes per shift at 30×30. Those per-shift numbers are the more useful planning figures because they already carry an assumption about real operating rhythm.

If a supplier quotes only cycle time, ask what tonnage per shift they expect on your material and with your loading method. If they quote only tonnes per shift, ask what cycle time and bale weight it assumes. The two should reconcile.

Shearing capacity, liner grade and the specs that decide durability

Three more lines deserve attention because they decide whether the machine survives your feedstock.

Shearing capacity. The High Density Baler is specified with shearing blades that handle material up to 20 mm; the Double Action Balers, in all three door configurations, are specified up to 5 mm. That is a fourfold difference and it is the line that tells you whether the press will cope with what you actually feed it. Oversized or thicker material either needs pre-cutting on a shear, or a press rated for it. The Super Jumbo Baler is specified to process heavy TMT bars 5–10 mm thick — that capability is what the extra cost buys.

Liner grade. Wear plate is graded by Brinell hardness. SSAB specifies Hardox 500 Tuf with a hardness window of 475–505 HBW and estimates its relative service life at 85–100% longer than Hardox 400. JHP specifies HARDOX 500-BHN wear plates throughout the High Density Baler and in the Super Jumbo compression chamber, and HARDOX 400 lining on the horizontal balers — harder liner where the material is metal, a lower grade where it is paper and plastic. A quotation that does not state liner grade is not comparable to one that does.

Foundation. The Double Action Balers in all three configurations, and the Vertical Baler, are specified as requiring no civil foundation. This never appears as a line item but it changes your project cost and your ability to relocate.

A sizing method you can run yourself

Work in this order. It takes ten minutes and it will stop you buying the wrong press.

  1. Fix the bale format. Ask your buyer — furnace, mill or trader — what bale size and weight they accept and what they pay for. Everything else is downstream of this.
  2. Establish your material mix. Ferrous, non-ferrous, mixed, ELV bodies or dry waste, in what proportion, and the thickest section you will feed. Indian scrap trades against recognised grades — HMS, LMS, shredded and similar — and BIS revised its code for processed ferrous scrap under IS 2549:2023, so the grade language in your purchase orders should match the grade language in your baler specification.
  3. Convert monthly tonnage to hourly. Monthly tonnes ÷ working days ÷ shift hours = tonnes per hour required. Add a growth allowance, but a modest one.
  4. Work backwards to bale weight and cycle. Required tonnes per hour ÷ realistic bales per hour = the bale weight you need — in your material, not in mild steel.
  5. Check the thickness line. If your thickest section exceeds the press's shearing capacity, you need either a shear ahead of the baler or a bigger press. Decide which is cheaper.
  6. Check the site. Floor area, headroom, ejection clearance, power supply and whether foundation work is possible at all.

If steps 3 and 4 land you between two models, take the smaller one and run a second shift before you take the larger one and run it half-empty. An underloaded press is capital sitting idle; a fully loaded smaller press is a machine earning.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between chamber size and bale size on a baling press? The chamber is the box you load loose scrap into; the bale is the compacted block that comes out. A High Density Baler with a 76×40×30-inch chamber produces an 18×18-inch bale. You select the bale format your buyer accepts and the chamber size follows from it.

Why is the bale weight lower for aluminium than for mild steel? Because aluminium is roughly a third the density of steel. The Triple Action Jumbo Plus 30×30 is specified at 800–1000 kg in mild steel and 250–350 kg in aluminium — the same machine and the same cycle. Always plan tonnage against the figure for your own material.

How do I calculate tonnes per hour from a specification? Divide bale weight by cycle time to get a theoretical rate, then discount it substantially for loading, strapping and bale removal. Where a manufacturer publishes tonnes per shift — as the Super Jumbo Baler does at 24–30 tonnes per 8-hour shift for 20×20 bales — that figure is the more realistic planning number.

What thickness of scrap can a baling press handle? It depends on the machine. The Double Action Balers are specified with shearing blades for material up to 5 mm, the High Density Baler up to 20 mm, and the Super Jumbo Baler is rated for heavy TMT bars 5–10 mm thick. Match this line to the thickest section in your feedstock before anything else.

Does a scrap baling press machine need a civil foundation? Not always. The Double Action Balers in all three door configurations and the Vertical Baler are specified as requiring no civil foundation, which makes them straightforward to install and relocate. Larger high-tonnage presses are a different case — ask specifically for the machine you are considering.
