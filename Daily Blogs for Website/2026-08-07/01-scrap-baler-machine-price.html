Scrap Baler Machine Price: What You Are Actually Paying For

Search for a scrap baler machine price and you will get a wall of marketplace listings quoting figures that range across an order of magnitude, most of them "price on request". That is frustrating, but it is not evasion. A baler is not a catalogue item — it is a configured machine, and two presses that look identical in a photograph can differ by a factor of three in cost because of what is inside them.

This article explains what those differences are, so you can read a quotation properly and compare two of them without being misled. We have not put invented rupee figures in it. What we have put in is the list of variables that decide the number, and a method for working out what your own machine should cost.

Why manufacturers do not publish price lists

Three reasons, and all of them are real.

Steel is the largest input, and steel moves. Structural plate, cylinder forgings and wear liner make up the bulk of a baler's material cost, so quotations are valid for a defined window rather than open-ended.

Almost nothing is standard. Chamber dimensions, ejection direction, door type, control system, motor rating and liner grade are all specified per customer. There is no single "20×20 baler" — there is your 20×20 baler.

Freight, civil work and installation vary by site. A press going 40 km to a yard with a crane on site costs a different amount to deliver and commission than the same press going 1,400 km to a plot with no hardstanding.

The seven variables that actually move the number

1. Press force

Force determines what you can compact and how dense the bale comes out. In the Jindal Hydro Projects range, the High Density Baler is specified with cylinder force up to 650 tons and the Super Jumbo Baler up to 300 tons. Higher force means a larger cylinder, a heavier frame to react against it, a bigger power pack and more steel throughout. It is the single biggest cost lever.

Buy the force you need, not force that sounds impressive. If your feedstock is light aluminium and UBCs, a 650-ton press is money spent on capability you will never use.

2. Chamber and bale size

Bale size drives chamber size, which drives frame size, which drives everything. The ladder is steep: across the High Density range the catalogue lists 18×18-inch bales from a 76×40×30-inch chamber at 120–140 kg in mild steel, up to 40×40-inch bales from a 110×60×55-inch chamber at 800–1000 kg.

Pick the bale size your buyer wants, not the biggest you can afford. If your furnace or trader takes a specific bale format, anything outside it is worth less at the weighbridge however impressive the press.

3. Motor rating and cycle time

These two are linked, and together they set your throughput. The Double Action Baler is specified at 15–20 HP across its three chamber sizes with cycle times of 50–85 seconds. The High Density Baler runs 60–120 HP with cycles of 50–120 seconds depending on model.

Cycle time deserves more attention than it usually gets. A machine that is 20% cheaper but 30% slower per cycle costs you more per tonne over its life, and the gap widens every year you run it.

4. Wear liner grade

This is where quotations diverge invisibly. Wear plate is graded by hardness in HBW. SSAB specifies Hardox 400 at a nominal hardness of 400 HBW, and states an 80–100% increase in wear life for Hardox 500 Tuf — 100 HBW harder — compared with Hardox 400.

JHP specifies HARDOX 500-BHN wear plates throughout the High Density Baler and in the Super Jumbo compression chamber, and HARDOX 400 lining on the Automatic and Semi-Automatic Horizontal Balers: harder liner where the material is metal, a lower grade where it is paper and plastic.

When comparing quotations, find the liner grade and thickness on both. If one does not state it, ask. A cheaper press with softer liner is not cheaper; it is a deferred bill.

5. Door, ejection and control

The same double-action press is offered in three configurations, and each one prices differently:

  • Manual door — hinged top door with a front hand-lever. Simplest and least expensive.
  • Auto door — PLC-controlled powered pressing door for hands-free loading. More components, more cost, less operator effort.
  • Top ejection — bale pushed out from the top, for sites where side or front clearance is limited.

Automation follows the same logic. The Triple Action Baler is offered with fully automatic PLC touch-screen control; the Vertical Baler with either electrical PLC or a hydraulic hand lever. PLC control costs more up front and pays back through consistency and reduced operator dependence — but only if you run the volume to justify it.

6. Foundation requirement

Several machines in the range — the Double Action Balers in all three configurations, the Vertical Baler, the Alligator Shear and the Nibblers — are specified as requiring no civil foundation. That is a real cost that never appears on the machine quotation: a designed foundation adds civil work, adds weeks to commissioning, and effectively fixes the machine in place. If you rent your yard or expect to relocate, weigh this heavily.

7. The options quoted separately

Online oil chillers, auto-lubrication, infeed and outfeed conveyors, magnetic separation, spare blade sets, extended warranty and AMC are usually line items rather than inclusions. Two quotations for "the same machine" can differ by a wide margin purely on what has been bundled. Ask each supplier for the same inclusion list before comparing totals.

How to compare two quotations honestly

Do not compare headline prices. Compare cost per tonne of installed capacity, then compare the things that will differ over ten years.

  1. Convert to throughput. Ask each supplier for bale weight and cycle time on your material, compute tonnes per shift, and divide the delivered installed cost by that. Now the numbers are comparable.
  2. Normalise inclusions. Add whatever one quotation bundles and the other does not to the cheaper quotation.
  3. Add ten years of consumables and power, using each machine's actual motor rating and stated blade life.
  4. Price the downtime risk. Where are spares held, what is typical dispatch time, who installs, and is there a service engineer within reach of your site?
  5. Check the specification is written down. Liner grade, plate thickness, cylinder make, pump and valve make, control system, warranty scope. If it is not in the quotation, it is not in the contract.

What to send a manufacturer to get an accurate quotation

You will get a firm number far faster if you supply this up front:

  • Material — ferrous, non-ferrous, mixed, ELV bodies or dry waste, and the thickest section you will feed
  • Volume — tonnes per month, and hours per day you will run
  • Target bale — size, weight and density your buyer accepts
  • Feeding method — manual, grab, loader or conveyor
  • Site — floor area, headroom, power supply, whether foundation work is possible
  • Constraints — ejection clearance, relocation plans, ambient temperature, shift length

That last point matters more than people expect. An online oil chiller is specified on several machines in the range precisely because long shifts in high ambient temperature are hard on hydraulic oil.

Frequently asked questions

Why will nobody give me a scrap baler machine price over the phone? Because the honest answer depends on your material, target bale size, throughput and site. A number given before those are known is a guess. Supply the six inputs listed above and a manufacturer can quote firmly rather than indicatively.

Is a more expensive baler always better? No. Paying for press force, bale size or automation you will not use is the most common and most expensive sizing error we see. The right machine is the one matched to your actual feedstock and volume, with a modest allowance for growth.

What GST applies to a baling press in India? Hydraulic presses fall under HSN heading 8462, with sub-heading 846291 covering hydraulic presses, listed at 18%. GST rates and classifications are amended from time to time, so confirm the current position on the CBIC rate finder or with your chartered accountant before you finalise a budget.

Do I need a civil foundation for a baling press? It depends on the machine. Several presses in the JHP range — including all three Double Action configurations and the Vertical Baler — are specified as requiring no civil foundation. Larger high-tonnage presses are a different case; ask specifically.

How do I work out running cost per tonne? Take motor rating in kW, multiply by running hours and your electricity tariff for the power line. Add blade and consumable replacement over a year, plus hydraulic oil changes, plus operator cost. Divide the annual total by annual tonnes baled. Do this for every quotation you are comparing — it frequently reverses the ranking.
