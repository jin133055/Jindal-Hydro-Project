Hydraulic Scrap Baling Press Machine: The Site, Power and Commissioning Checklist

Most of the delay and most of the unbudgeted cost in buying a hydraulic scrap baling press machine happens after the order is placed and before the machine makes its first bale. The press is built to a schedule. The site rarely is.

The pattern is consistent: the machine ships, arrives, and then waits — for a foundation to cure, for a discom to sanction extra load, for a panel that was never ordered, or for a shed door two feet too narrow. None of these are engineering problems. They are all things that could have been settled at the time of order.

This is the checklist to work through the week you place the order, not the week the truck arrives. If you want to understand the hydraulics themselves rather than the site, our piece on how a baling press hydraulic system works and what goes wrong covers that ground.

Foundation: find out early whether you need one

The foundation line is the single biggest swing in installation cost, and it is entirely knowable in advance.

Several machines in the JHP range are specified in the catalogue as requiring no civil foundation: all three Double Action Baler configurations — auto door, manual door and top ejection — and the Vertical Baler. The Alligator Shear and the hydraulic and mechanical Nibblers are also specified this way. That removes a civil contractor, a curing period, and a permanent commitment to one spot on your floor.

Larger, higher-tonnage presses are a different matter, and you should ask the question explicitly rather than assume either way.

Be clear about what "no civil foundation" does and does not mean. It means the machine does not need a purpose-designed reinforced concrete block sized to its dynamic loads. It does not mean any floor will do. You still need a sound, level surface that carries the static weight without settling, enough slab thickness to take repeated cyclic loading without cracking at the feet, and drainage or a bunded area, because hydraulic machines eventually leak something.

If your floor is a thin screed over made-up ground, that is worth knowing before the machine is on a truck.

Power: connected load is not sanctioned load

Motor ratings in a catalogue are given in horsepower. Your electricity board deals in kilowatts. The conversion is simple — HP × 0.746 = kW — and doing it early avoids the most common surprise on the whole project.

Worked from the JHP catalogue figures:

Vertical Baler (hand lever models) 10 ~7.5
Double Action Baler 15–20 ~11–15

Triple Action Baler, Jumbo Plus 30×30 60×2 ~90

That is the press alone. Add conveyors, lighting, a shear, welding sets and anything else on the same connection, then compare the total against your current sanctioned load. If you are short, the upgrade has a cost and a lead time — and the lead time is usually the part that hurts.

Three more electrical items to settle before delivery:

  • Cable sizing and run length. Voltage drop over a long run to a yard corner is a real effect, not a theoretical one. Size for the actual route.
  • Earthing. The Central Electricity Authority's safety regulations cover earthing arrangements and earth-leakage protection; published summaries of the 2010 regulations describe a requirement for earthing via two separate connections and for a suitable earth-leakage protective device on installations with load above 5 kW exceeding 250 V. Treat that as an indication of what your contractor must design to, and have the actual design signed off by a licensed electrical contractor against the current regulations and your state's requirements.
  • Panel location. Somewhere dry, accessible, and not where a loader will reverse into it.

Space: the chamber is not the footprint

Buyers routinely plan floor space from the chamber dimensions in the specification table. The chamber is the hole the scrap goes into. The machine around it, plus the power pack, plus the space a person needs to stand, is considerably larger.

Ask for a general arrangement drawing before you plan the layout, and check four clearances against it:

  1. Charging clearance. Top-loading machines need vertical room above the chamber for the grab or magnet to open. Measure to the lowest obstruction — usually a roof truss or a crane girder, not the roof sheet.
  2. Ejection clearance. Where the bale comes out decides where you need room. The Double Action Baler in top-ejection configuration exists precisely because some sites have no side or front clearance to give. Choose the configuration that fits the building you have.
  3. Maintenance access. Cylinder removal, blade changes and hose replacement all need room on a specific side. Ask which side.
  4. Bale handling and despatch. Bales stack, and a stack needs floor area and truck access. A 30×30 bale in mild steel from the Super Jumbo range weighs 700–800 kg; plan handling for that, not for the average bale.

Oil, heat and the Indian ambient

Hydraulic systems are sensitive to two things your site controls: temperature and cleanliness.

On temperature, an online oil chiller is specified on several machines in the range — the Mini Triple Action Baler carries an online oil chiller and auto lubrication, and both the Automatic and Semi-Automatic Horizontal Balers are specified with an online oil chiller, with the automatic machine rated to run up to 20 hours. If you are running long shifts through an Indian summer in an enclosed shed, cooling is not an optional extra, and shed ventilation belongs in the same conversation.

On cleanliness, contamination is the leading cause of premature hydraulic component failure, according to filtration specialist Donaldson Hy-Pro, which recommends monitoring fluid against the ISO 4406 standard. ISO 4406 expresses cleanliness as a three-number code — for example 19/17/14 — counting particles per millilitre at 4, 6 and 14 microns, on a logarithmic scale where each step up roughly doubles the particle count range. The same source makes the point that new oil is not necessarily clean oil.

The practical version for a new installation: agree who supplies the first fill, insist the system is flushed before commissioning, get a target cleanliness code in writing, and fit a decent reservoir breather on day one rather than after the first pump failure.

Safety and statutory items that are yours, not the supplier's

Guarding and statutory compliance sit with the occupier of the factory, and they are not something to sort out after the first month of production.

Section 21 of the Factories Act, 1948 requires that every part of an electric generator, motor or rotary converter, every part of transmission machinery, and every dangerous part of any other machinery be securely fenced by safeguards of substantial construction, and that those safeguards be constantly maintained and kept in position while the parts they fence are in motion or in use. Factory rules, licensing and inspection are administered at state level and vary, so confirm the position with your state factory inspectorate.

Alongside that, settle before commissioning: emergency stops within reach of every working position, a written lockout and isolation procedure for blade changes and chamber clearing, operator training records — JHP supplies operator training with installation as standard — and fire provision appropriate to hydraulic oil rather than a general extinguisher.

Commissioning: what to test before you sign off

Commissioning is the last point at which you have leverage. Use it. A reasonable acceptance test for a hydraulic scrap baling press machine covers:

  • Cycle time at rated pressure, using your material — not a demonstration material. Compare against the quoted cycle.
  • Bale weight and dimensions, measured over several consecutive bales rather than one.
  • Oil temperature rise over a continuous run of at least an hour, with the chiller running if fitted.
  • Current draw at no load and under full compression, recorded, so you have a baseline for later fault-finding.
  • Leak check on every joint after the system has been to full pressure and back several times.
  • Every emergency stop and interlock, tested individually.
  • Documentation handover: hydraulic circuit diagram, electrical schematic, recommended spares list, preventive-maintenance schedule.

Record the results. Two years later, when the cycle has crept from 80 to 100 seconds, that commissioning sheet is the only way to know whether the machine has drifted or your material has changed.

Frequently asked questions

Does a hydraulic scrap baling press machine need a concrete foundation? It depends on the machine. In the JHP range, all three Double Action Baler configurations — auto door, manual door and top ejection — and the Vertical Baler are specified as requiring no civil foundation, as are the Alligator Shear and the Nibblers. Larger high-tonnage presses may require one. Ask the manufacturer explicitly at enquiry stage rather than assuming.

How much electrical load should I plan for? Convert the motor rating using HP × 0.746 = kW, then add every other load on the same connection. A 120 HP press is roughly 90 kW on its own. Compare the total against your sanctioned load and start any upgrade with your discom early, because the approval lead time is usually longer than the machine's delivery time.

How much space do I need around the press? More than the chamber dimensions suggest. Request a general arrangement drawing and check charging clearance above the chamber, clearance in the ejection direction, maintenance access on the side where cylinders and blades are serviced, and floor area for stacking and loading bales. Top ejection exists specifically for sites with limited side or front clearance.

What hydraulic oil cleanliness should I ask for? Ask your supplier for a target ISO 4406 code and for confirmation that the system will be flushed before commissioning. Donaldson Hy-Pro notes that contamination is the leading cause of premature hydraulic component failure and that new oil should be filtered before it enters the system. Fitting a good reservoir breather from day one is cheap insurance.

What should I check before signing the commissioning certificate? Cycle time at rated pressure using your own material, bale weight and dimensions over several consecutive bales, oil temperature rise over a continuous run, current draw at no load and under compression, a full leak check, every emergency stop tested individually, and complete documentation. Record everything — it becomes your performance baseline.
