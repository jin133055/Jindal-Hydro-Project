Scrap Bundling Machine: What the Term Means, and Which Press Makes Your Bundle

Ask ten people in the Indian scrap trade what a scrap bundling machine is and you will get at least four answers. Some mean a baling press. Some mean the car baler that squashes an end-of-life vehicle into a solid block. Some mean the wire-tying head that straps an already-compressed bale. And some mean a rebar bundling line in a rolling mill, which is a different industry altogether.

That ambiguity costs money. Enquiries go to the wrong manufacturer and quotations come back for the wrong machine. This article sorts out what the term covers, then works backwards from the bundle you have to sell to the press that makes it.

The four things people call a bundling machine

1. A baling press. By far the most common meaning. Loose scrap goes into a compression chamber, one or more hydraulic rams compress it, and a solid bale — a bundle — comes out. Everything in the sections below is about this machine.

2. A car baler or ELV compactor. A specialist press for end-of-life vehicles. In the JHP catalogue this machine is described as compacting entire car bodies, doors, and bus and truck parts into solid high-density bundles. The word "bundle" is literal here, and it is the machine most Registered Vehicle Scrapping Facilities have in mind when they say bundling.

3. A strapping or tying machine. This does not compress anything. It binds material that is already cut or already compressed, using wire or strap. If your material is already the right size and you only need it held together for transport, you do not need a press at all.

4. A rebar or rod bundling machine. Downstream finishing equipment in a rolling mill, which collects, counts and ties finished TMT bars into commercial bundles. This is not scrap processing equipment, and a scrap baling press will not do that job. If that is what you are after, you are looking in the wrong equipment category — and it is worth saying so plainly rather than quoting you a press.

If you are in category 4, stop reading. Everyone else, keep going.

Start with the bundle, not the machine

The single most useful thing you can do before shortlisting a scrap bundling machine is to find out exactly what your buyer wants to receive.

Bale specification is set downstream, not by you. An induction furnace has a charging bucket or a charge door with fixed internal dimensions — a bale that will not fit has to be re-cut, and you pay for that twice. A rolling mill may want a specific weight per bundle so that its handling equipment can lift it. An exporter cares about density, because container weight limits decide whether the load is profitable.

So the questions to ask your buyer are:

  • What maximum bale dimensions can your charging system take?
  • What bale weight suits your crane, magnet or grab?
  • Do you discount loose or low-density bundles, and by how much?
  • Do you have a minimum density you will accept?

Get those four answers in writing and the machine selection almost makes itself. Skip them, and you risk buying a press that produces a technically excellent bale nobody in your area wants to buy.

Matching the press to the material

Once you know the bundle, the press has to be able to make it out of your material. Two properties matter most: bale weight in mild steel, and whether the machine can shear or accept the section thickness you are feeding.

Cut first, or press first?

A press is not always the first machine in the line. If your intake is long section — round bar, channel, TMT, angle, cables, plates or tubes — it usually has to be cut before it will charge cleanly into a chamber.

That is what an alligator shear is for. The JHP range is specified with shear forces from 35 to 200 tons, cutting capacities up to 85–120 mm round, 7–14 strokes per minute, and no civil foundation required. Buying a bigger press to swallow uncut material is almost always more expensive than buying a modest shear to cut it first.

What to have ready before you ask for a quotation

Manufacturers can only quote firmly against a specification. Have these eight things written down and the process gets much faster:

  1. Material type and grade mix
  2. Maximum section thickness you will feed
  3. Tonnes per month, and shifts you are willing to run
  4. Bale dimensions and weight your buyer wants
  5. Feeding method — manual, conveyor, grab or magnet
  6. Available power, and current sanctioned load
  7. Floor conditions and whether civil work is possible
  8. Whether you need ejection from the top, front or side

Any manufacturer who quotes without asking most of these is quoting a catalogue number, not your machine.

The demand backdrop, with appropriate caution

Two policy currents sit behind the current interest in bundling equipment, and both are worth watching without over-reading.

On the ferrous side, the Ministry of Steel is finalising a national steel scrap recycling policy to replace the 2019 guidelines. Speaking in January 2026, Joint Secretary Daya Nidhan Pandey said scrap availability is estimated to rise to nearly 36 million tonnes, and that India aims to raise the share of scrap in steelmaking towards the global average of 31% as capacity moves towards 300 MT by 2030. The policy contours had not been disclosed at that point.

On the vehicle side, the RVSF framework gives owners a Certificate of Deposit that can be traded on the NCDEX platform, along with a 15% motor vehicle tax rebate for transport vehicles, 25% for non-transport vehicles, and a full waiver of registration fee on a new vehicle. Those incentives shape how much material reaches formal facilities.

Both are reasons to plan, not reasons to buy capacity you cannot fill today. Confirm current policy positions with the Ministry of Steel and MoRTH respectively, and size your machine against the material you can actually source next quarter.

Frequently asked questions

Is a scrap bundling machine the same as a baling press? In most Indian usage, yes — people say bundling machine and mean a hydraulic baling press that compresses loose scrap into a solid bale. The exceptions are car balers, which produce ELV bundles, strapping machines, which only tie material, and rebar bundling lines in rolling mills, which are unrelated equipment. Confirm which one is meant before comparing quotations.

How do I choose the bale size? Work backwards from your buyer. The charging system at the furnace or mill sets the maximum dimensions, and their handling equipment sets a practical weight. Once you know both, pick the smallest machine in the range that comfortably makes that bale at your required tonnes per hour.

What is the difference between double action and triple action? Double action compresses from two directions and suits lighter, thinner material in a compact footprint. Triple action compresses from three directions — top, sideways and longitudinally — which produces denser bundles from springy or irregular material such as auto skeletons, wires and offcuts. Triple action costs more and is worth it only if your material needs it.

Do I need a shear as well as a press? If your intake includes long section such as round bar, channel, TMT or tubing, usually yes. An alligator shear cuts material to a size that charges cleanly, which lets you buy a smaller press. Feeding long uncut section into an undersized chamber damages the machine and slows the cycle.

Which machine makes bundles from whole vehicles? A car baler, also called an ELV compactor, is purpose-built for this and compacts entire car bodies, doors and bus or truck parts into solid high-density bundles. It is a standard part of the equipment line at a Registered Vehicle Scrapping Facility. Capacity options are configured per site, so specifications are quoted against the plant rather than listed.
