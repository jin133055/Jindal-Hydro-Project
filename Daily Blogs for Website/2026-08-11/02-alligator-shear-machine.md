# Alligator Shear Machine: How to Size One, and When You Need Something Else

An **alligator shear machine** — also called a crocodile shear or lever shear — is usually the first cutting machine a scrap yard buys, and it is often bought a size too large. The reasoning is understandable: someone shows up with a 100mm round bar once a month, so the buyer specifies for that. The result is a machine that costs more, draws more power, and spends most of its life cutting 40mm material it did not need 200 tonnes of shear force to handle.

This article explains what the spec sheet numbers mean, how to size the machine from the material you actually process, and when a nibbler or a box shear is the better answer.

## What the machine does, and what it does not

An alligator shear is a hinged-jaw cutting machine. The upper blade pivots down against a fixed lower blade, and material is cut by shearing rather than sawing or burning. There is no heat, no consumable gas and no kerf loss.

In the JHP catalogue it is specified for cutting round bar, channel, TMT, angle, cables, plates and tubes, with a broad shearing jaw for wide scrap and cutting capacities up to 85–120mm round depending on model. Operation is available as manual, full-auto or foot-pedal, and the machine needs no civil foundation.

That last point matters more than most buyers realise. A machine that needs no foundation can be positioned where the material is, moved when the yard layout changes, and commissioned in days rather than weeks — a real advantage if you are still settling the layout of a new Registered Vehicle Scrapping Facility.

What an alligator shear does **not** do well is cut thin sheet cleanly. It deforms it. If your material is sheet, see the comparison below before you go further.

## The five numbers that actually decide the machine

Here is the technical specification table from the catalogue:

| Shear force (T) | Jaw opening (in) | Cutting capacity (mm) | Strokes/min | Motor (HP) | Weight (kg) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 35–50 | 9 | 40–50 round | 10–14 | 15–40 | 2,800–6,500 |
| 65–100 | 9–12 | 65–80 round | 7–14 | 40 | 6,500 |
| 85–200 | 12–16 | 85–120 round / 75 sq | 7–12 | 40–60 | 8,000 |

Read it in this order.

**Cutting capacity is the headline, and it is stated in round bar.** A quoted capacity of "80mm round" means solid round bar in mild steel. It is not 80mm of anything. Hollow section, layered plate, hardened bar and stacked material all behave differently, and the honest way to establish what your material needs is a cut trial, not a conversion table.

**Jaw opening decides what fits, not what cuts.** Two different constraints. A wide channel or flattened tube may be well within the shear force rating and still not enter a 9-inch jaw. If your material is bulky rather than thick, jaw opening is the binding constraint and you should size for it directly.

**Strokes per minute is your real throughput number.** Notice the pattern in the table: strokes per minute *falls* as the machine gets bigger, from 10–14 at the smallest range down to 7–12 at the largest. Buying a heavier machine than you need does not just cost more — it can cut fewer pieces per hour.

**Motor rating is your running cost and your connection.** The range is 15 HP to 60 HP. Before committing to the top of it, check your sanctioned load. A machine you cannot power on your current connection turns into an electricity board application with its own timeline.

**Weight is a proxy for build, and a logistics problem.** From 2,800 kg to 8,000 kg. Heavier generally means a more rigid frame and better blade life under load, but it also decides how the machine gets to site and what handles it there.

## Size it from your regular cut, not your worst case

The sizing method that works is unglamorous. Spend a week logging what actually goes through the yard: section type, dimension, and how much of each. Then take the **90th percentile** of that log, not the maximum, and size the machine to cut that comfortably.

For the occasional oversize piece — the one 110mm bar a month — you have cheaper options than a bigger shear: cut it with gas, send it out, or ask the supplier to deliver it shorter. Any of these beats buying two size classes up and running under-loaded all year.

Two adjustments. If your intake is genuinely unpredictable — mixed demolition scrap, or ELV material where you take what arrives — bias upward, because you cannot log what you cannot predict. And if you are shearing to feed a baling press, size the shear so its output charges your press chamber cleanly. A shear producing pieces your press cannot swallow has solved nothing.

## Alligator shear, nibbler or box shear

These get confused in enquiries constantly, and the wrong choice is expensive.

**Alligator shear** — mixed structural section. Round bar, channel, TMT, angle, cable, plate, tube. Fast, flexible, no foundation. Deforms thin sheet.

**Nibbler** — sheet, where deformation matters. The catalogue specifies a V-shaped blade for clean, precise cuts with minimum deformation, in hydraulic and mechanical variants, cutting material up to 62–80mm thick and needing no civil foundation. The hydraulic nibbler runs 14–16 strokes per minute on a 15 HP motor with a 4-inch blade opening, 50–80mm round cutting capacity, 2,400 PSI working pressure and a machine weight of 2,500 kg. Note that it is *faster* than every alligator shear in the table above and lighter than all of them. If your material is sheet and section, the honest answer is often that you need both machines rather than one compromise.

**Box shear or inclined shear** — car bodies and heavy structural vehicle scrap. Different machine class, sized against whole assemblies rather than section. In the catalogue's ELV plant line-up, the inclined or box shear handles car bodies while the alligator shear handles round bar, channel, TMT and structural parts recovered from vehicles. They are complementary, not alternatives.

If a supplier quotes you one machine for all three jobs, ask them to be specific about which compromise you are accepting.

## What to check before you sign

- **Blade material, hardness and reversibility.** Blades are the consumable. Ask how many cutting edges each blade has, what it costs, and what the lead time is. A cheap machine with a six-week blade lead time is not cheap.
- **Jaw opening confirmed against your bulkiest material**, measured, not estimated.
- **Strokes per minute at load**, not free-cycling. Ask for the figure under the material you actually cut.
- **Motor rating against your sanctioned load**, with starting current accounted for.
- **Hydraulic system serviceability.** Where are the valves and pump made, and can you get them locally?
- **Guarding and emergency stop.** An alligator shear is one of the more dangerous machines in a yard. Specify guarding and train operators before commissioning, not after an incident.
- **A cut trial on your own material** before payment terms are finalised. Any manufacturer confident in the machine will agree to one.

## Why the demand for sized scrap is holding up

Two currents sit behind current interest in cutting equipment, and both deserve a hedge.

Sizing changes what you get paid. Clean, sized, correctly separated material sells against a different line on the buyer's rate card than mixed scrap does — published Delhi market rates on 10 August 2026 show how wide the spread runs between fractions ([Shree Metal Prices](https://shreemetalprices.com/delhi-scrap-prices-today/)). That is a daily trade snapshot, not a planning figure, but the direction is consistent: a shear pays for itself through grade discipline more than through tonnage.

On the policy side, India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme is bringing energy-intensive sectors including iron and steel under intensity-based emission targets, with compliance years FY2025-26 and FY2026-27 and targets notified in stages from 2025 ([ICAP](https://icapcarbonaction.com/en/news/india-notifies-emission-intensity-targets-nine-sectors-under-carbon-credit-trading-scheme)). A framework that prices emission intensity per tonne of product tends to favour the scrap-fed electric route over the coal-based one — a reason to expect steady scrap demand, not a reason to buy capacity you cannot feed. Confirm the current notification status with the Bureau of Energy Efficiency and MoEFCC before treating it as a business case. Separately, the vehicle scrapping programme continues to push ELV material into formal facilities, where structural parts must be cut before they can be baled or sold ([PIB](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleaseIframePage.aspx?PRID=1705811&reg=48&lang=2)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is an alligator shear machine used for?**
It cuts scrap metal to size by shearing between a hinged upper blade and a fixed lower blade. In the JHP range it is specified for round bar, channel, TMT, angle, cables, plates and tubes, and is used in scrap yards, recycling plants, ELV facilities and fabrication shops. It does not cut thin sheet cleanly — a nibbler is the right machine for that.

**What cutting capacity do I need?**
Log a week of actual material and size for the 90th percentile, not the largest piece you have ever seen. Catalogue models cover 40–50mm round, 65–80mm round and 85–120mm round or 75mm square, with shear force from 35 to 200 tonnes. Handle the rare oversize piece another way rather than buying two size classes up.

**Does an alligator shear need a foundation?**
No. The catalogue specifies no civil foundation required, for both the alligator shear and the nibbler. That shortens commissioning and lets you reposition the machine as the yard layout changes.

**Why do bigger shears have fewer strokes per minute?**
Larger jaws and longer strokes take more time per cycle. Across the catalogue range strokes per minute falls from 10–14 on the smallest models to 7–12 on the largest. For high-volume small-section cutting, an oversized machine will cut fewer pieces per hour than a correctly sized one.

**Alligator shear or nibbler — which do I need?**
Section versus sheet. Alligator shear for structural material where some deformation is acceptable; nibbler for sheet where a clean, low-deformation cut matters. The catalogue's hydraulic nibbler runs 14–16 strokes per minute on a 15 HP motor at 2,400 PSI, with a 4-inch blade opening and 50–80mm round cutting capacity. Yards handling both material types often need both machines.

**Can an alligator shear cut car bodies?**
Not efficiently. Whole car bodies and heavy structural vehicle scrap call for a box or inclined shear. In an ELV or RVSF plant the two work together — the box shear handles bodies, the alligator shear cuts round bar, channel, TMT and structural parts recovered from the vehicles.
